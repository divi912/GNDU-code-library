The Reality Check: DSA vs. Development

Here is the reality check about the industry. It is basically split into two parallel universes: Data Structures & Algorithms (DSA) and actual Software Development. Imma be real about DSA no sugarcoating, it is essentially the JEE of the software world. (though dont take that as a bad thing) It is a highly competitive, quantifiable rat race meant to test pure logical reasoning. It doesn't really teach you how to build a scalable application, but it is a necessary grind if your target is GAMMA (Google, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon) or any Tier-1 company like that I’m sure uk about them all. On the flip side, development is where the actual soul of programming lives. It is about building silly things, breaking systems, and exploring your creativity. But relying entirely on development to get hired is a massive gamble/risk because it is completely subjective. You could build a masterpiece, and an interviewer might still struggle to compare you objectively to someone else who built something completely different (diff idea, diff execution and even different language). That is exactly why I called DSA quantifiable earlier on as when companies default to DSA it is incredibly easy to quantify who solved 600 questions versus who solved 100. Tier 1 companies see just DSA, Tier 2 need 80% DSA and 20% Dev (im excluding soft skills like communication btw) and even Tier 3 need 60% ish DSA and even that 40% Dev which is left over can be EASILY covered by building very small scale simple projects that you can honestly just vibe code (more on that later). No one’s really gonna check your projects code to see how secure or scalable that is and also yeah the companies know that if you have a good DSA profile that means you do know your way around logics and problem solving, and they’ll teach you the dev stuff like their own language, their own infra and etc etc.

Startups are the only companies that usually do not ask for DSA, they require people that can join their team and build stuff straight away but ofc the job market is tough rn so you gotta stand out and be different to be even considered.

Domain Dictates Language

One of the biggest rookie mistakes I see juniors make is asking, "Bhaiya, should I learn Python or Java?". That is the wrong question entirely. The golden rule is that the domain dictates the language, not the other way around. You need to choose what you want to build first, and then pick the language that rules that specific space. If you decide you want to build AI and Machine Learning models, then Python is king. If you want to build interactive Frontend web applications, you simply have to learn JavaScript. Locking yourself into a language before you even know what you want to build is only going to restrict your future choices.

The "T-Shaped" Developer

Before we get to this concept, let's talk about the "Full-Stack" myth. Calling yourself a full-stack developer in your second or third year is honestly a trap. If you split your focus that early, you end up with half the depth of someone who specialized. Y

You need to become a "T-Shaped" developer instead. Imagine the alphabet T in your mind okay?

The vertical bar represents your depth, THE ONE main area where you are highly confident and practically unbeatable.

For eg for me, that vertical bar is Backend Engineering then the horizontal bar represents your breadth, these are the adjacent branches where you know just enough to build personal projects or help out specialists in your team. So again for eg while my depth is backend, I still know enough about DevOps, AI Agents, and basic frontend to piece an entire project together or atleast help out in that if I’m in a team.

Build your depth first, and then expand horizontally.

When you are picking that depth, understand what you are actually getting into. 

NOW JUST FOR EG, take Web Dev/App Dev - there we have 2 things - Frontend (what u see) and Backend (what actually happens behind the scenes)

Frontend is NOT JUST making things look pretty with UI/UX design. Half of frontend development is pure logic i.e handling complex states, managing form submissions, dealing with redirects, and figuring out how to fetch and render data in a way that actually makes sense to the user.

Backend, on the other hand, is the magic that no one ever sees. The thrill of backend engineering comes from anticipating insane edge cases, building robust message queues, managing CDNs, optimizing your database queries, and ensuring the entire system can actually scale efficiently when thousands of users hit it at once. AI in the Developer Workflow

Finally, we need to have a very real talk about AI.

AI has drastically lowered the barrier to entry for coding, literally anyone can generate a basic app nowadays with no prior knowledge BUT it has absolutely not lowered the barrier to true engineering, like handling scalability, robustness, and massive user traffic.

My core stance for you is that you have to use AI responsibly. Let it be your tool, but do not let it turn you into its tool.

I need to give you a serious warning here: there is an incredibly thin line between using AI to accelerate your workflow and using it as a crutch that stops you from actually learning.

That line is getting thinner and thinner every single day.

You have to be self-aware enough to identify that boundary and stop yourself WHEN you cross it (not if, because there will definitely be times when you’ll be crossing the boundary out of lazziness) because if you blindly rely on it, it will quietly eat away at your foundational problem-solving skills. If you just blindly copy and paste from it, you become a passenger in your own codebase.

At the same time, you cannot afford to live in denial. Refusing to use AI will leave you completely in the dust.

Why?

Because if you stubbornly refuse to use it out of some sense of traditional pride, someone else out there is going to use it to become five times more productive than you. When it comes down to it, they are going to get the job over you.

You simply have to use AI (responsibly); it is non-negotiable in today's landscape.

But there is ABSOLUTELY NO NEED to be terrified of it, either.

You will hear a lot of panic online about AI causing massive industry layoffs. While it is true that AI can now essentially do the baseline coding work of a junior developer, it is not the entire reason layoffs are happening. Macroeconomic factors like recessions and market corrections play a huge role, and AI is very often just being used as a convenient scapegoat by corporations.

So how do you survive and thrive in this environment?

By stepping up from a junior coder to an actual engineer.

AI can quickly write the implementation, but the architecture, planning, system design, and security reviews are entirely on you.

An AI might be able to scaffold a basic backend in seconds, but designing how those components interact, how the database handles heavy load, and how secure the endpoints are is completely your job.

IF YOU DO NOT sit down, read, and fundamentally understand every single line of code the AI generates, you WILL lose control of your project within a matter of months. And TRUST ME, when that complex system eventually breaks and the obscure bugs start piling up, neither you nor the AI will be able to save the codebase.

Let me give you a very real timeline to put this into perspective.

Back in the pre-LLM era i.e just a couple of years ago if you were picking up a new technology to build a project, it would lets just for the sake of this example say take you about one week just to learn the core concepts, and then another two weeks of tedious, manual typing to actually implement it.

Here is the reality of how that has changed today: you absolutely still need that one full week to learn. Even if you use AI to explain the theory or break down complex documentation, it still takes time for your brain to absorb the architecture and the logic.

AI cannot digest that information into your brain for you.

But those two weeks of practical implementation?

That time has been cut down by MORE THAN HALF

YES! And that incredibly fast timeline includes the essential, non-negotiable time you must spend reading, reviewing, and fully understanding the code the AI generated for you.

You get to skip typing out the repetitive boilerplate, but you only get that massive speed boost if you spent that first week actually learning the fundamentals. If you try to skip the learning phase, you will just end up spending those saved weeks helplessly debugging a massive wall of code that you don't even understand.
